Chapter 1 of 4

Meet the ecosystem

Before we listen to the radar, let’s meet the place it is watching: the mangroves of the Ciénaga Grande de Santa Marta, on Colombia’s Caribbean coast.

What is this place?

The Ciénaga Grande de Santa Marta is a large coastal lagoon system on the Caribbean coast of Colombia, where fresh water from rivers meets salt water from the sea. forests grow along its shores and channels.

Why do mangroves matter?

Mangroves are trees that live where land and sea meet. Their roots hold the shore, shelter young fish and birds, and store carbon in their wood and soil. NASA describes mangroves as a critical coastal ecosystem under severe threat from climate change, and one that responds quickly to change, which makes it a useful indicator.

[Verify: add one or two local facts, e.g. fisheries, species, nearby communities.]
Who depends on them?

Fishing communities, coastal families and wildlife all depend on healthy mangroves and healthy water flow.

[Verify: name the communities and number of people only if a source confirms it.]
What can go wrong?

Mangroves need the right mix of fresh and salty water. When water flow changes, or when trees are cut or flooded for too long, patches of forest can thin out or die back.

[Verify: add the documented history of the Ciénaga Grande with a source.]
Why use radar here?

This coast is often cloudy and humid, and the mangroves sit in a landscape of water, mud and trees that changes with tides and seasons. Radar can look through clouds and, with the right wavelength, tell water, dense mangrove and sparse mangrove apart.

Chapter 2 of 4

Listen to the signals

Radar doesn’t take pictures. It sends a pulse and listens to the echo. Let’s learn to read that echo.

What is NISAR?

NISAR is a satellite built by NASA and the Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO). It was launched on July 30, 2025, and it carries a synthetic aperture radar () to measure changes in Earth’s land, ice, water and vegetation.

It carries two radars: an radar (about 24 cm wavelength) and an S-band radar (about 9.4 cm). It observes nearly all of Earth’s land and ice surfaces twice every 12 days.

What is radar?

A camera collects light that already exists. Radar is an active instrument: it sends its own energy and measures the signal that returns, called .

Because it brings its own signal, radar works at night and through clouds. The “synthetic aperture” part means the satellite combines many echoes along its path to make a sharp image.

Strong echo = bright pixel. Weak echo = dark pixel.

Watch a pulse leave the satellite, hit the ground and return.

Reading the tones

In a radar image, brightness tells you how much signal came back, not what color things are.

Bright areas return a strong echo: rough surfaces, tall vegetation, buildings. Dark areas return a weak echo: calm water and bare, smooth ground.

Tone is a clue. We combine tones, polarizations and dates to read the landscape.

Very dark

Calm open water. The pulse bounces away like a mirror.

Medium gray

Low or sparse vegetation, bare soil.

Bright

Dense vegetation, tall trees, or water under trees (the pulse bounces twice).

Polarizations

Radar waves can vibrate horizontally (H) or vertically (V). A tells you which way the wave was sent and which way it was received.

HH: sent horizontal, received horizontal. It reacts strongly to smooth surfaces and to the between water and trunks.

HV: sent horizontal, received vertical. It reacts to the tangled volume of leaves and branches, so it is a good signal for how much vegetation there is.

Each polarization is a different voice. Together, they tell a fuller story.

Mangroves from space

Radar can “feel” three kinds of echo in a mangrove forest. Calm water reflects the pulse away. Trunks and roots standing in water can bounce it twice. The canopy of leaves and branches scatters it in many directions.

Because these echoes behave differently in HH and HV, we can tell water, dense mangrove and sparse mangrove apart.

How each number was calculated

  • HectaresPixels of each class are counted and multiplied by the area of one pixel. [Pixel size]
  • Mean HV (dB)Average HV backscatter of the mangrove area in each scene, in decibels.
  • Decrease / stable / increasePixels whose HV changed by more than [threshold] dB between Date A and Date B are marked as decrease or increase; the rest are stable.
  • Confidence levelBased on [season difference, water/tide conditions and data quality]. High, medium or low.

Limitations

  • Short time seriesWe only have 16 dates, so we cannot see long-term trends yet.
  • Water and tidesWater level and tides change the radar signal in a place with so much water. Some changes may come from water, not from the trees.
  • Approximate classificationOur classes come from simple thresholds, not from field surveys. Useful for exploring, not for official measurements.
  • No ground truth hereWe have not compared our results with field data. Only field or optical data can confirm what happened on the ground.
