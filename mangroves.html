Chapter 1 of 4

Meet the ecosystem

Before we listen to the radar, let’s meet the place it is watching: the mangroves of the Ciénaga Grande de Santa Marta, on Colombia’s Caribbean coast.

The Ciénaga Grande de Santa Marta is a brackish coastal lagoon in Magdalena, Colombia. Its balance depends on fresh water from the Magdalena River and Sierra Nevada rivers meeting salt water from the Caribbean Sea. It is the largest and most productive coastal lagoon in the Colombian Caribbean.

26,810 haFlora & Fauna Sanctuary
1998Colombia’s first Ramsar wetland
2000UNESCO Biosphere Reserve
53+fish species

Mangroves

Red, black, white and button mangroves. Their roots shelter fish, crabs, molluscs and nesting birds.

Wildlife

Manatees, otters, howler monkeys, caimans and huge flocks of migratory ducks from November to April. Even the Caribbean flamingo is back.

Climate

27–30 °C all year. Rains from May to November; dry season from December to April, when evaporation raises salinity.

What can go wrong?

When fresh water stops flowing, salinity rises. Mangroves die back and fish die, mostly in February and March.

Created by INDERENA in 1977 (23,000 ha) and expanded in 1998 to 26,810 ha. Only conservation, research, education and control activities are allowed. Sources: Minambiente, Parques Nacionales, Colparques.

Chapter 2 of 4

Listen to the signals

Radar doesn’t take pictures. It sends a pulse and listens to the echo. Let’s learn to read that echo.

What is NISAR?

NISAR is a satellite built by NASA and the Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO). It was launched on July 30, 2025, and it carries a synthetic aperture radar () to measure changes in Earth’s land, ice, water and vegetation.

It carries two radars: an radar (about 24 cm wavelength) and an S-band radar (about 9.4 cm). It observes nearly all of Earth’s land and ice surfaces twice every 12 days.

LaunchedJuly 30, 2025
PartnersNASA and ISRO
RadarsL-band and S-band
DataOpen and free

NISAR is like a planet-sized listening device that never needs sunlight.

What is radar?

A camera collects light that already exists. Radar is an active instrument: it sends its own energy and measures the signal that returns, called .

Because it brings its own signal, radar works at night and through clouds. The “synthetic aperture” part means the satellite combines many echoes along its path to make a sharp image.

Strong echo = bright pixel. Weak echo = dark pixel.

Watch a pulse leave the satellite, hit the ground and return.

Reading the tones

In a radar image, brightness tells you how much signal came back, not what color things are.

Bright areas return a strong echo: rough surfaces, tall vegetation, buildings. Dark areas return a weak echo: calm water and bare, smooth ground.

Tone is a clue. We combine tones, polarizations and dates to read the landscape.

Very dark

Calm open water. The pulse bounces away like a mirror.

Medium gray

Low or sparse vegetation, bare soil.

Bright

Dense vegetation, tall trees, or water under trees (the pulse bounces twice).

Polarizations

Radar waves can vibrate horizontally (H) or vertically (V). A tells you which way the wave was sent and which way it was received.

HH: sent horizontal, received horizontal. It reacts strongly to smooth surfaces and to the between water and trunks.

HV: sent horizontal, received vertical. It reacts to the tangled volume of leaves and branches, so it is a good signal for how much vegetation there is.

Each polarization is a different voice. Together, they tell a fuller story.

Real NISAR scene of the Ciénaga Grande, October 29, 2025. Radar has no color: our code paints each echo strength with a color scale so you can read it.

Mangroves from space

Radar can “feel” three kinds of echo in a mangrove forest. Calm water reflects the pulse away. Trunks and roots standing in water can bounce it twice. The canopy of leaves and branches scatters it in many directions.

Because these echoes behave differently in HH and HV, we can tell water, dense mangrove and sparse mangrove apart.

Chapter 4 of 4

Discover the evidence

Here is how we got from satellite data to the numbers and stories you saw. Nothing is hidden.

NISAR data

We use 16 NISAR scenes of the Ciénaga Grande, freely available through the Alaska Satellite Facility.

Processing

Our Python pipeline reads each scene, uses the HH and HV polarizations and prepares them for comparison. Each scene is cropped to the study area, averaged in 4×4 pixel blocks to reduce speckle, and converted to decibels (dB).

Computed results

A pixel is open water when its HV signal is below −20.78 dB (a threshold found automatically on the Oct 29, 2025 scene). The code compares pairs of dates in hectares.

AI explanation

A language model turns only those computed results into plain language. It never sees raw data and is not allowed to add numbers.

How each number was calculated

  • HectaresPixels of each class are counted and multiplied by the area of one pixel. Each pixel is 20 m × 20 m, so 0.04 ha.
  • Vegetation HV (dB)Median HV backscatter of the area that was never water on any date (stable vegetation), in decibels.
  • Became / stopped being waterPixels that were not water on Date A and are water on Date B “became water”, and the reverse. Patches under 1 ha (25 pixels) are ignored as radar noise.
  • Confidence levelWe compare the change with the normal change between two passes 12 days apart, with the same processing version. The bigger the difference, the more likely something really changed.

Limitations

  • Short time seriesWe only have 16 dates, so we cannot see long-term trends yet.
  • Water and tidesWater level and tides change the radar signal in a place with so much water. Some changes may come from water, not from the trees.
  • Approximate classificationWater comes from a single threshold, not from field surveys. Useful for exploring, not for official measurements.
  • No ground truth hereWe have not compared our results with field data. Only field or optical data can confirm what happened on the ground.
